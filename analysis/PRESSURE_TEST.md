# Pressure test of the inherited-401(k) handoff

Reviewed against tax-year 2026 statute. **The core thesis survives. Most of the
supporting numbers do not, and the largest single item is missing entirely.**

Verdict up front: keep the 2–3% mortgage and fund the deficit with controlled
withdrawals. That was the right call — but it was reached partly by accident,
and the analysis as framed would size the withdrawals wrong.

---

## 1. Errors that change the answer

### 1.1 The tax illustration counts payroll tax on retirement distributions. It doesn't apply.

The figures ($86K → ~$15K, $110K → ~$22K, $240K → ~$70K "federal + Georgia +
payroll") imply a marginal rate of **(70 − 22) / 130 = 37%** on the distribution
dollars between $110K and $240K.

Retirement-plan distributions are **not wages**. No Social Security tax, no
Medicare tax, no additional Medicare tax. The true marginal cost of a
distribution dollar is:

> federal marginal rate + 4.99% Georgia — and nothing else.

For MFJ/QSS in 2026 that range sits in the 22–24% federal bracket, so the real
marginal cost is **≈27–29%**, not 37%. The illustration overstates the cost of
withdrawing by roughly a third.

This matters in both directions. It makes a large withdrawal look worse than it
is, and it makes "spread it thin" look better than it is. The right conclusion
was reached on a wrong premise.

### 1.2 Georgia has no brackets to manage.

Georgia is a **flat 4.99% in 2026** (HB 463; the phase-down from 5.19% completed
this year). There is no Georgia progressivity. Every "spread income across years
to stay in a lower state bracket" argument is void here — only the federal
brackets respond to timing.

Related, and useful later: Georgia excludes **$35,000** of retirement income at
age 62–64 and **$65,000** at 65+. She is 52, so she gets nothing now — but this
is a real reason to shift withdrawals *later* rather than earlier, and it points
the opposite way from the bracket-filling instinct.

### 1.3 The 10-year rule does not apply to her.

The handoff lists "10-year-rule implications" as something to analyze. A
surviving spouse is an **Eligible Designated Beneficiary** and is not on a
10-year clock. She can take life-expectancy distributions.

Better still: under **SECURE 2.0 §327** (effective 2024), a sole surviving-spouse
beneficiary may elect to be treated as the participant — deferring RMDs until the
decedent *would have* reached his RMD age, then using the more favorable Uniform
Lifetime Table. This is a planning asset the handoff had filed as a risk.

Practical consequence: **nothing forces money out of this account for years.**
That single fact defeats most of the case for accelerating withdrawals.

### 1.4 Every dollar figure in the tax table was built on the wrong filing status.

The handoff notices this itself, but then keeps the numbers. They should be
discarded, not adjusted. Correct sequence, assuming she does not remarry and the
child remains a dependent:

| Year | Status | 2026 standard deduction | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 (year of death) | **Married filing jointly** | $32,200 | Includes his pre-death income |
| 2027, 2028 | **Qualifying Surviving Spouse** | $32,200 | MFJ brackets, requires dependent child |
| 2029 → child turns 19 | **Head of Household** | $24,150 | Narrower brackets |
| after that | **Single** | $16,100 | The "widow's penalty" |

## 2. What the handoff got right — and it's the most important point in it

**The penalty analysis is correct and it is the highest-value item in the
document.** Confirmed:

- Distributions from the decedent's plan, or from a properly titled **inherited
  (beneficiary) IRA**, are exempt from the 10% early-distribution penalty under
  **IRC §72(t)(2)(A)(ii)** — the death exception — at any age.
- Rolling the money into **her own IRA** converts it to her money and re-imposes
  the 10% penalty until 59½. She is 52. That's a **7½-year trap**.

The instinct to keep the funds in beneficiary form while she still needs access
is exactly right. In the model this optionality is worth little if everything
goes to plan (~$6K) and a great deal if it doesn't (~$217K if she ever needs a
large withdrawal). **It costs nothing to preserve. Preserve it.**

She can always roll to her own IRA after 59½. She can never undo it before.

## 3. What's missing — and one omission is bigger than the mortgage question

### 3.1 Social Security survivor benefits are not mentioned once.

This is the gap that matters. With a dependent child and a deceased insured
worker:

- **The child** receives **75% of his PIA** until age 18 (19 if still in high
  school). This is not affected by her earnings.
- **She** receives **75% of his PIA at any age** — no age-60 wait — while caring
  for his child **under 16**. This is the "mother's benefit."
- Both are capped by the **family maximum**, roughly 150–188% of PIA.
- **Catch:** her own benefit is subject to the retirement earnings test — **$1
  withheld for every $2** earned above **$24,480** (2026). On a normal salary her
  caregiver benefit is likely withheld in full. **The child's benefit is not.**
- Also: the **$255 lump-sum death payment**, and her own **widow's benefit at 60**
  (reduced) or full retirement age.

In the model this is worth roughly $27K/year — which is most of the "cash-flow
gap" the whole exercise is trying to solve. **File with SSA before modeling
anything.** Survivor benefits are generally payable only ~6 months retroactively;
delay is permanently lost money.

### 3.2 The stepped-up taxable assets should be spent first.

Any taxable brokerage or jointly held investments received a **basis step-up at
death**. Selling them now realizes little or no gain. These are the cheapest
dollars she owns, and they were not on the strategy list.

**This was the best-performing strategy in every single sensitivity run** —
better than gap-funding by ~$84K, purely by changing *which account* the money
comes from. It requires no forecast to be right.

### 3.3 The deficit is a step function, not a level.

Under the placeholder inputs there is **no cash-flow deficit at all** until the
child's survivor benefit ends and she drops to Single filing — then it appears
abruptly. Paying off the mortgage today spends the most expensive dollars she has
to solve a problem whose peak is years away and whose shape is completely
different from a level shortfall.

### 3.4 Her housing cost falls on its own at 62.

**Cobb County grants a 100% school-tax exemption at age 62, with no income
limit.** School tax is typically ~55% of a Cobb bill. Marietta city has its own
$4,000 homestead exemption and a separate age-62 school exemption. Also: the
homestead exemption must be **re-filed in her name** after the transfer.

### 3.5 Do not refinance. Recast if needed.

A recast keeps the rate and maturity and only re-amortizes the payment — that is
the right tool. A **refinance would destroy the 2–3% rate** and is the single
worst available move.

She also does not need to pay off or refinance to keep the house:
**Garn-St Germain (12 U.S.C. §1701j-3(d))** bars the lender from calling the loan
on transfer to a surviving spouse, and **CFPB successor-in-interest rules** give
her the right to loan information and to assume the loan.

### 3.6 Smaller items that were left out

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Life insurance proceeds** | Income-tax-free. If material, it may moot the entire question. |
| **Employer group life / pension survivor annuity** | Frequently overlooked; check his HR file. |
| **NUA on employer stock** | If the 401(k) holds appreciated employer stock, net unrealized appreciation gets long-term capital-gain treatment. Potentially very large — and **destroyed by an unplanned rollover**. Check before moving anything. |
| **Mandatory 20% withholding** | 401(k) plan distributions carry mandatory 20% federal withholding. A $320K payoff requires a **~$400K gross request**. Inherited IRAs default to 10% and are electable — an operational reason to move to an inherited IRA. |
| **Does the plan allow partial distributions?** | Many plans force a lump sum on beneficiaries. If so, that alone dictates a direct transfer to an inherited IRA — before any tax discussion. |
| **Georgia year's support petition** | State-law claim available to a surviving spouse and minor child; time-limited. |
| **Child tax credit** | $2,200 for 2026, phasing out above $400K MFJ — a large distribution can burn it. |

### 3.7 Two items in scope that should be dropped

- **Medicare / IRMAA**: irrelevant. She is 52; IRMAA's two-year lookback first
  bites at 63.
- **ACA subsidies**: irrelevant *if* she has employer coverage. Only re-enters if
  she loses it.

## 4. Where I'd push back on my own addition

The standard move for a widow is to **fill the wide MFJ/QSS brackets in
2026–2028** before the "widow's penalty" narrows them. I modeled it (Strategy E).

**It loses — by roughly $220K.**

The widow's penalty is real, but it is outweighed here by two things: she has
a decade-plus of low-income years ahead, and there is **no 10-year rule forcing
distributions**, so deferred growth compounds untaxed for a long time. Pre-paying
tax at 22% + 4.99% to avoid a later 24% simply isn't worth it at a 6% return.

This would flip if the balance were large enough to force big RMDs later, or if
her income is set to rise sharply. Worth re-running with real numbers — but the
reflex should be resisted, not followed.

- Paying off the mortgage only wins if the portfolio returns **less than 1.73%**.

assumptions — the ranking holds across every tested value of mortgage rate,
portfolio return, salary, 401(k) balance, his PIA, and mortgage balance.

## 6. Recommended sequence

1. **File with SSA immediately.** Survivor benefits are limited to ~6 months of
   retroactivity. This is the largest and most time-sensitive item.
2. **Do not move the 401(k) into her own IRA.** Keep it in the plan or transfer
   directly to a properly titled **inherited IRA**. Check for employer stock
   (NUA) *before* any transfer.
3. **Confirm the plan permits partial distributions.** If it doesn't, transfer to
   an inherited IRA is forced — do it as a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer.
4. **Keep the mortgage.** Recast only if the payment is genuinely unaffordable
   after steps 1–3. Never refinance.
5. **Re-file the Cobb County homestead exemption** in her name; calendar the
   age-62 school exemption.
6. **Spend stepped-up taxable assets before 401(k) dollars.**
7. **Then, and only then,** size annual withdrawals against the real deficit —
   which may be near zero until the child ages out.

The withdrawal-optimization question the handoff ends on is the *last* question,
not the first. Steps 1–3 are worth more than any withdrawal schedule, and two of
them expire.

## 7. Confidence and caveats

- Statutory figures (brackets, deductions, GA rate, SS rules, penalty exceptions)
  are 2026 and were verified against source; they are in `taxes.py` and are not
  assumptions.
- Everything in `inputs.py` is a placeholder. **No dollar figure in this document
  should be quoted to anyone** until the intake checklist is filled in.
- The model is deterministic — a fixed return, no sequence-of-returns risk, no
  market crash in year one. A Monte Carlo layer would sharpen the payoff-vs-invest
  margin but will not reverse a 1.73% break-even.
- Not modeled: state estate/probate mechanics, NUA, ACA, and any life insurance.
- **This is analysis, not advice.** The account-titling decision in §2 and the
  NUA question in §3.6 are irreversible and are worth a CPA or fee-only fiduciary
  review before anyone signs a form.
