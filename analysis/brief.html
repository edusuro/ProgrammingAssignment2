What to do with the retirement account

There is a lot going on right now, so this is only about the 401(k) — nothing else. There are really just two questions, and both have clear answers.

The short version

  1. Leave the account in his name. Moving it into her name would cost around $30,000 in penalties, and it can be moved later for free.
  2. Don't pay off the house. Doing that costs about $92,000 over ten years. Take a little out each year instead.

That's the whole thing. Nothing has to be decided this week, and nothing needs to come out of the account this year at all.

Question 1

Should the account be moved into her name?

No — not for another seven years.

Money inherited from a husband or wife can be taken out at any age with no penalty. That protection belongs to the account, not to her. The moment it moves into her own name it becomes her retirement money, and anything taken out before she turns 59½ gets hit with a 10% penalty. She's 52.

There are a few small advantages to having it in her own name. All of them are still available at 59½, and moving it then costs nothing. So this isn't really a trade-off — it's just a reason to wait.

What that looks like in practice: the account simply stays where it is at TIAA, with her as the beneficiary — that's Option 1 on the form. The paperwork confirms she can request whatever amount she needs, whenever she needs it, so there's no reason to move the money anywhere.

Careful — TIAA's own letter points the wrong way. It says her next step is to "transfer ownership of the account(s) to your name." Read plainly that is the $30,000 move, and it's written as though it were routine housekeeping. Their own form contradicts it in the small print.

Accepting the inheritance does not require putting the accounts in her name. That's a separate box, and it's the one to decline. If anyone at TIAA presses, the question to ask is: "does that forfeit the early-withdrawal exception before I turn 59½?"

And if money ever does move between companies, it has to go directly from one to the other. A check written to her personally would make the whole balance taxable in a single year.

Question 2

Should she cash it out to pay off the house?

No. Paying it off now costs about $92,000 over ten years.

The house is the reason this feels urgent, but the mortgage isn't really the problem. It's at about 2.5% — remarkably cheap — and with only ten years left, most of each payment is already going to principal rather than interest. All the interest left on the loan adds up to roughly $22,000.

To clear it today she'd have to pull about $233,000 out of the account, because around $67,000 of that goes straight to taxes. Taking it all in one year pushes her into a much higher tax bracket than spreading it out would.

Between the last two options it's genuinely her call — they're only about $6,500 apart.

Take a little each year is the simplest thing to do: pull just enough to cover the mortgage, leave everything else alone.

Take more in 2027–28 uses a tax break she qualifies for as a widow. For those two years only, money taken out is taxed at roughly 17% instead of 27%. Taking about $48,000 in each of those years and putting the extra straight onto the loan pays the house off in 2033 instead of 2035, and comes with the lowest tax bill of all three options.

It finishes about $6,500 behind — a small price if having the house paid off sooner would take some weight off her shoulders. There is no wrong answer between these two.

Either way the mechanics are easy: the $611,410.93 is held in ordinary investments, with none of it tied up in an annuity that pays out on a fixed schedule. She can take a different amount every year without difficulty.

Worth knowing

Paying off the house wouldn't end the monthly payment

The $2,205.16 that goes out each month isn't all mortgage. About $1,577 of it is the loan; the other $628 is property taxes and insurance, collected along with the payment — roughly $7,500 a year.

That part continues for as long as she owns the house. So even if the mortgage were cleared tomorrow, the monthly bill wouldn't disappear — it would drop by about 72%, not to zero. Better to know that before the decision than after.

The details behind all this

Why 2027 and 2028 are cheaper

For the year someone dies, the surviving spouse still files jointly. For the two years after that, if there's a dependent child at home, she can file as a Qualifying Surviving Spouse — which keeps the wide married-couple tax brackets while only her single income fills them. That's what creates roughly $48,000 of room taxed at about 17% in each of 2027 and 2028. From 2029 she files as Head of Household and that room shrinks to about $4,000; from 2033 she's back to 27% on the first dollar.

Two things would close that window: the child no longer qualifying as a dependent, or remarrying before the end of 2028. Both worth confirming.

The 20% withholding rule

Money coming out of an employer 401(k) has 20% held back automatically and sent to the IRS. This is not an extra tax — it's a prepayment, credited on her tax return and refunded if too much was taken. It just means that to actually receive $10,000 she has to request about $12,500, and the excess sits with the IRS until she files. An inherited IRA doesn't work this way: withholding starts at 10% and can be adjusted or switched off. It's a convenience, not a reason to pick one over the other.

An option we left out

There's a fourth variation — take more in 2027–28, but put the extra into savings rather than onto the loan. It came out about $3,900 ahead of "take a little each year" over ten years, which is close enough to nothing that it isn't worth the added complexity. Noted only so it doesn't look overlooked.

Which plan this probably is

Georgia Tech faculty are in the University System of Georgia. Salaried faculty choose, irrevocably, between the Teachers Retirement System (a traditional pension) and the Optional Retirement Plan — a savings account held at TIAA. His letter shows two TIAA contracts and two CREF certificates, which usually means the ORP plus a supplemental 403(b). Worth asking whether any Teachers Retirement System service also exists, since that would carry a separate monthly survivor benefit.

None of this changes the two answers. Inherited money from a spouse is penalty-free in all of these, and all of them can move to an inherited IRA if that turns out to be simpler. What it changes is the paperwork, the beneficiary check, and whether a lump sum is even available.

What we assumed

Confirmed: $167,332 left on the mortgage with about ten years to run, a $2,205.16 monthly payment, her salary of $86,000, his $80,000 earned in 2026, and a $611,000 retirement account.

Confirmed since: the account is $611,410.93, all in CREF and mutual funds, and he was 50 — so nothing is forced out of the account until roughly 2051.

Assumed: a 2.5% mortgage rate, a 6% return on money left invested, 3% on money set aside in savings, and her salary rising about 3% a year. The tax rules are 2026 law — federal brackets, Georgia's 4.99% flat rate, and the penalty exemption for inherited accounts.

The 2.5% rate checks out. At that rate the loan portion of the payment is $1,577.44, leaving about $628 of taxes and insurance — right for a house of this value in Cobb County. Even if the real rate is 3%, the total interest changes by only about $4,600, which doesn't affect any of the conclusions.
